# How to read a paper you cannot fully understand

This course has no prerequisites and assigns research papers. Those two facts
are in tension, and this page is how we resolve it.

You will be assigned papers containing mathematics you have not studied. This is
not an oversight. Learning to extract the argument from a paper whose technical
core is beyond you is a genuine skill, it is one practitioners use constantly,
and you can start building it now.

---

## The rule for this course

**Every paper on the schedule tells you which parts to read.** Look for the
:material-information-outline: note under the reading on the session page.

When it says "Abstract, Introduction, and Section 3," that is the assignment.
Reading further is optional and reading less is not. You are never expected to
work through mathematics that has been marked optional.

---

## Three passes

### First pass — five minutes

Read the **title**, **abstract**, and **section headings**. Look at the
**figures**, especially the first one, which is usually the architecture or the
main result.

Then answer one question: *what is this paper claiming?* If you cannot say it in
a sentence, read the abstract again. Do not proceed until you can.

### Second pass — twenty minutes

Read the **introduction** properly and the **conclusion**. Skim the assigned
body sections. Skip proofs, skip derivations, skip anything marked optional.

Now answer three questions:

1. What problem existed before this paper?
2. What did the authors do about it?
3. What evidence do they offer that it worked?

Most classroom discussion happens at exactly this level. If you can answer these
three, you can participate fully.

### Third pass — only if you want to

Follow the mathematics. Work an example. Look up the cited papers. Do this when
a paper matters to your project, not because it was assigned.

---

## What to do when you hit a wall

**Note it and keep going.** Do not stop at the first unfamiliar symbol.
Unfamiliar notation is usually load-bearing for a proof, not for the argument.

**Read the figure caption instead.** Captions in ML papers frequently explain in
two sentences what a section takes two pages to establish.

**Find the explainer.** Jay Alammar's [Illustrated
Transformer](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/) exists because
the Vaswani paper is hard. That is why it is assigned first, in Week 3. Where an
explainer exists for an assigned paper, it is on the schedule.

**Ask a model — carefully.** Asking Claude to explain Section 3.2 is a
legitimate use and one this course endorses. Asking it to summarise the paper
so you can skip reading it is the failure mode described in the [AI-use
policy](../syllabus.md#using-ai). The difference is whether you
end up understanding the paper or understanding a summary of it, and you will
know which happened when I ask you about it in class.

**Bring the wall to class.** "I did not follow Section 4" is a useful
contribution, not an admission. Someone else did not follow it either.

---

## What you are actually being assessed on

Not whether you understood the mathematics. Whether you can state:

- what the paper claims,
- what evidence supports it,
- and what would have to be true for the claim to be wrong.

That third one is the hard one, and it is the one this course cares about most.
It is also what makes Week 6 work — you will read chain-of-thought prompting
and the paper arguing it is a mirage, and the assignment is not to decide who is
right but to describe what would settle it.
